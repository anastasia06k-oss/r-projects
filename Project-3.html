Part I

In this project we want to look at the way life satisfaction is associated with household income and how this association is moderated by social and political trust in Italy. Previous research (Clench-Aas & Holte, 2021) suggests that 2/3 of variance in the life satisfaction in European countries stem from income (personal, community and national). This association was significantly modified by trust, and all 3 forms of trust (personal, social, political) played the role of “buffer” moderating the relationship between life satisfaction and income. Findings showed that higher trust levels reduce the strength of relationship between income and life satisfaction. Thus, in high-trust contexts income matters less for individuals while in low-trust context, on the contrary, income is important and might strongly predict life satisfaction.

For the sake of feasibility, we will not look at all the levels studied in the mentioned article. We decided to limit our analysis to three continuous predictors, namely trust in parliament (political trust), social trust and household income.

The results of multilevel meta-analysis conducted by Bi et al.(2025) showed a positive overall association between trust and subjective well-being (relying on the article by Helliewell and Putnam (2004) we imply that “life satisfaction” is one of the measures of subjective well-being). It supports the expectation, that people who report higher levels of trust may also report higher life satisfaction. The authors noted that there were variations in the strength of association depending on the type of trust and other factors. Nevertheless, the results of longitudianl meta-analyses revealed that well-being predicts subsequent trust and vice versa.

At the same time, Glatz and Schwerdtfeger (2022) show that the causal relationship between social trust, institutional trust and subjective well-being is complicated. Their findings suggest that well-being may also have an inmact on trust. Taking into account the previous research, the chosen variables can be considered theoretically justified predictors for life satisfaction.

According to the sjPlot output and model2 summary, we report the following results: for 1834 observations in the data set, R2 equals 0.095 (meaning that the model explains around 9,5% of variance), R2 adjusted equals 0.093, AIC equals approx. 7282. The intercept is 5.63 (p< 0,001), indicating the expected level of life satisfaction for individuals in the reference category of education (higher education) when all continuous predictors are equal to zero. The coefficient for income (hinctnta) is positive and statistically significant (b = 0,124, p < 0,05), which means that an increase in income by one unit is associated with an increase in life satisfaction by 0,124 units, while other variables are constant. The magnitude is small. The coefficient for general social trust (ppltrust) is positive and statistically significant (b = 0,059, p<0,05), the increase in one unit of general trust is associated with the increase in life satisfaction by 0,059 units. The magnitude of the effect is small. The coefficient for parliament trust (trstprl) is positive and statistically significant (b = 0,097, p<0,05). The increase in parliament trust by one unit is associated with the increase in life satisfaction by 0,097 units. The magnitude is small. The negative relationships between individuals with medium and higher education is not statistically significant (b = -0,027, p > 0,05), indicating no meaningful difference in life satisfaction between these groups. The magnitude is small.

Conclusion

This project examined the associations between life satisfaction and three key predictors: household income, generalized trust and trust in parliament, using data from the European Social Survey (ESS Round 11) for Italy (N = 1,834). Addressing previous research (Clench-Aas & Holte, 2021; Bi et al., 2025), we hypothesized positive correlations between each predictor and life satisfaction, as well as positive intercorrelations among the predictors themselves.

All six hypotheses were supported, although the strength of associations turned out to be relatively weak.

Proceeding with the main model, we compared several nested models for linear regression and compared their performance. The best model fit included the following predictors: income (by deciles), general_trust, parliament_trust and education (by categories).

The model explained 9.5% of the variance in life satisfaction (R2 = 0.095, F = 38.59, p < .001). Though R2 appears to be small, we suppose that a concept of life satisfaction is too complex to be fully predicted by the specified predictors, so more in-depth, multi-factor analysis is required.

Parliament and generalized trust, income and contrast between lower and higher education showed significant results, with education being the most powerful predictor in the model (est. Beta = -0.28). Medium education vs. higher education showed no significant difference (est. Beta = -0.03, p = .62). Gender did not improve model fit (F-test between model2 and model3: p > .05) and was remowed from the final model. Income × gender interaction was also not significant (model4).

The key result is that income, generalized trust and parliament trust are each positively associated with life satisfaction in Italy, with lower education (but not medium education) showing a meaningful negative effect.

For future research, it is better to include more predictors to achieve higher variance explanation and increase power of the model, as well as address the issue of causal direction between life satisfaction and trust, where each variable can affect each other.
